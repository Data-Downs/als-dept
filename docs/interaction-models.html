Four interaction models for the citizen app

Exploring how identity-first, conversation-first, and feed-first architectures could reshape the way citizens interact with government services — and a recommended hybrid that takes the best of each.

Where we are today

Tab-based, section-by-section, service-oriented

The current app uses a traditional four-tab structure: Home, Chat, To-do, and Wallet. Despite having transformational agentic capabilities — life event detection, cross-service field merging, delegated action, proactive eligibility — the interaction model still organises itself around government's taxonomy. Services live in one tab, conversations in another, tasks in a third. The agentic loop is fractured across views.

Recommended

The Mirror

The Mirror's identity-first foundation + the Journal's conversation depth + the Stream's story progression

You open the app and see your life — your family, your money, your health, your vehicles. Government is invisible infrastructure. Conversations open from data points and live inside your life facets, with the depth and persistence of full threads. Life event journeys unfold as swipeable stories. No tabs. No service directory. Just you, your circumstances, and an agent that wraps government around your needs.

From the Mirror
Your life is the home screen
Life facets — my family, my money, my vehicles — organised around YOUR categories, not government departments. Data-driven alerts surface what needs attention.
From the Journal
Conversations with depth
Conversations persist, can be resumed, and carry full context — inline service cards, consent panels, task cards, outcome cards. Not disposable sheets.
From the Stream
Story progression for journeys
Life event plans unfold as swipeable full-screen stories. Each service is a card you swipe through — satisfying, informative, and more engaging than a list.

Why this combination? People don't open a government app to browse or scroll a feed. They come because something happened — they're pregnant, someone died, a deadline is approaching. They come with purpose, often stressed, often unfamiliar with what's available. The Mirror starts from that truth: here's your life, here's what needs attention, here's what you're entitled to that you're not getting.

But a pure Mirror risks shallow interactions. Contextual sheets that disappear after use lose the continuity that complex government journeys demand. That's where the Journal's conversation depth matters — when Priya talks about Universal Credit, that conversation persists inside her "my money" facet. She can find it, resume it, see the history. The context surrounds the conversation.

And the Stream's story progression makes life events — the most transformational capability in the app — feel tangible and satisfying. Swiping through "Having a baby" and seeing each service as a full-screen card, with status and outcomes, is fundamentally more engaging than a checklist.

  • Works for everyone. A 19-year-old new mum sees "my family" and "my money" and immediately understands. A 72-year-old widower sees an alert. No text box to stare at, no feed to scroll past.
  • Solves discoverability. Services surface as "you could get" suggestions anchored to your circumstances inside facets — not as a catalogue to browse or a prompt to guess.
  • Handles cold start. Even with minimal data, "my family", "my money", "my health" are meaningful categories. The structure exists before the data does.
  • Matches intent. People come to government with purpose. The alert model — red dot on vehicles, amber suggestion on money — matches episodic, purposeful engagement.
  • Closest to the current code. The data, APIs, and orchestration layer are untouched. It's a frontend refactor of how the same features are assembled, not a rebuild.

The three alternatives explored

Each of these models contributed ideas to the recommended hybrid above. They're shown here in full to illustrate the thinking behind each approach and the trade-offs involved.

Model 1

The Journal

Like Claude + iMessage + a case worker's notebook

Plans, tasks, credentials, and consent all live inside conversations, not in separate tabs. There is no service directory because you never browse; you just talk. The agent initiates threads proactively when something needs attention.

All four models share one principle: the tab bar is gone. Government's taxonomy is invisible. Conversations are contextual, not isolated. And the agentic loop — from life event detection through to credential issuance — flows naturally rather than being fractured across separate views.

The recommended Mirror hybrid takes the strongest idea from each model: identity-first from the Mirror, conversation depth from the Journal, story progression from the Stream. The organising principle is simple: your life comes first, government wraps around it.
