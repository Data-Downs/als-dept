GOV.UK One Login vs Government Gateway

The state of play in 2026 — two parallel login systems, no clean cutover, and why a citizen genuinely cannot tell which one they need.

Compiled July 2026 · Grounded in gov.uk guidance, GDS blog posts, and departmental sources · A living snapshot of a mid-rollout situation

The short version: the confusion citizens feel is not a personal failing. It is structural. The UK runs two authentication systems side by side — the older Government Gateway and the newer GOV.UK One Login — and which one you need depends on the service, on whether you are a new or existing customer, and on a migration that has barely begun. The same person routinely holds both, plus an NHS login, plus others, and no screen ever explains the difference.

Why this matters for the project

This login mess is the on-ramp to the whole thesis. A citizen who cannot work out which login to use — and is asked to create yet another one, and prove their identity, mid-crisis — is exactly the citizen who will hand their credentials to a confident agent that says “don’t worry, I’ll deal with it.” To simulate that honestly, the personas must carry this same mess.

Two systems, side by side

The old one

Government Gateway

Introduced in the early 2000s. A sign-in only system: a 10–12 digit user ID and a password. Historically it did not prove who you are with documents — it used knowledge-based checks (questions about your own data).

Still the primary route for HMRC — Self Assessment, PAYE, VAT, Making Tax Digital — and used by other services too. A single person can accumulate several Gateway IDs (personal tax, self-employment, a company/organisation), because different registration journeys mint new ones.

The new one

GOV.UK One Login

Run by the Government Digital Service. Sign in with an email and password, plus — where a service needs it — real identity verification (scan a passport or driving licence and take a face/liveness photo).

Intended as the single “front door” that will eventually replace Government Gateway, the retired GOV.UK Verify, and individual departmental logins. Emphasis on eventually: there is no hard switchover.

Confirmed. gov.uk guidance states One Login “over time…will replace all other sign in routes including Government Gateway.” The multiple-Gateway-IDs phenomenon is indicative — widely reported but not nailed to a single primary source in this research pass.

Why it confuses — three concrete failures

1. HMRC’s sign-in screen offers three doors

Since 9 February 2026, signing in to HMRC presents a choice of Government Gateway, GOV.UK One Login, or “Create new sign-in details.” A citizen with no way to know which applies to them is asked to pick — and picking wrong sends them down a dead end.

2. The migration has barely started

One Login went live for HMRC on 9 February 2026, but only for a proportion of brand-new customers. Every existing Government Gateway holder keeps using their Gateway ID, with no action required and no citizen-facing deadline. Bringing existing users across is the next phase — not started, with a soft internal target around 2027 and a Gateway switch-off floated for the end of the parliament (~2029).

3. You can hold both — for different services

gov.uk’s own words: a person may have a GOV.UK One Login for the State Pension or Companies House and still need to use Government Gateway for HMRC “for now.” Same citizen, two logins, split by service, with nothing to reconcile them. This is the single clearest cause of “which login do I use?”

The tell: in the user research behind this project, citizens could not name which login belonged to which service — and neither, initially, could the team. That is the design failing the simulation must reproduce, not smooth over.

The GOV.UK One Login app & identity verification

There is a dedicated GOV.UK One Login app, and almost nobody understands what it is for — because it does two unrelated jobs.

  • Proving your identity: scan an accepted photo ID (passport, driving licence) and take a face/liveness photo; the app checks the ID is real, that you are a real person, and that you match it.
  • Generating security codes: it also acts as an authenticator, producing the codes you enter when signing in.

It replaced the older “GOV.UK ID Check” app for most users — but ID Check still exists for HMRC / Government Gateway checks. So there are two apps, doing overlapping things, for adjacent systems.

Three ways to prove identity

  1. The One Login app — document scan plus face photo.
  2. Security questions online — checked against data held by Experian and DWP (this route went live around mid-February 2026).
  3. In person at a Post Office — around 4,500 branches, for people who cannot use the app or online route.

This document-and-biometric proofing is the real functional difference from Government Gateway (knowledge-based checks) and from the retired GOV.UK Verify. It is also a genuine barrier: being asked to create a One Login and prove your identity is a hard step to face in the middle of, say, a bereavement.

The wider credential sprawl

Gateway and One Login are only two of the logins a single person accumulates. A realistic portfolio spans several unconnected accounts.

  • One or more Government Gateway IDs — personal tax, and possibly self-employment or a company.
  • GOV.UK One Login — for newer services; may or may not have identity verified.
  • NHS login — separate again, for health services.
  • The HMRC app — sits on top of Gateway/One Login.
  • Personal Tax Account — reached via HMRC sign-in.
  • Universal Credit / DWP sign-in — its own journey.
  • Individual local-council accounts — council tax, parking, bins, one per council.

Indicative. The specific relationships among NHS login, the Personal Tax Account and the HMRC app were not each pinned to a primary source in this pass — treat the sprawl as directionally accurate and worth confirming before any claim leaves the building.

What this means for the simulation

Personas cannot carry a single tidy login. Each needs a credential portfolio that reflects the mess: the Gateway ID(s) they hold, whether they have a One Login and whether its identity is verified, and the separate NHS login — plus a note on their particular confusion.

Worked example — Sarah Okafor

  • A Government Gateway login her late husband set up in 2019 for the tax on his pension. Familiarity: hazy.
  • No GOV.UK One Login at all — she will have to create one, and prove her identity, for the bereavement services.
  • A separate NHS login.

So a single bereavement journey drops her between a Gateway login she barely remembers (probate, inheritance tax) and a One Login she does not have yet (Tell Us Once, bereavement support). That wall — mid-grief — is precisely what makes “just let the agent do it” feel like relief rather than risk. The simulation exists to make that moment visible.
